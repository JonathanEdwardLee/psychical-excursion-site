# Psychical Excursion — Author Voice

## Purpose

This file captures Jonathan Lee's voice, personal experiences, worldview, humor, recurring questions, and reasons for creating *Psychical Excursion*.

It is not a source of scientific fact. Personal experiences should be presented as personal experiences. Historical, scientific, religious, occult, and cultural claims used in the book should be researched and cited separately.

## Core voice

Curious before certain.

Playful without being flippant.

Skeptical without making skepticism the point.

Open to mystical traditions without requiring mystical belief.

Friendly, nonjudgmental, conversational, and occasionally funny.

Never preachy.

Never “this is the one true way.”

Comfortable saying:

- Maybe.
- I don't know.
- Let's try it.
- What did you notice?
- Maybe it's happening entirely in the mind. That's still interesting.
- Maybe different traditions are describing similar experiences with different models.
- We don't have to decide what something is before we explore it.

A major influence is Robert Anton Wilson's model-agnostic attitude: something can be entertained as true without demanding that it be accepted as absolute truth.

A recurring principle:

**What we believe may be less important than what we actually experience.**

## Why I am doing this

I am not writing this because I have mastered astral projection.

I haven't.

I have lucid dreamed. I have experienced consciousness remaining alert while my body appeared to be asleep. I have practiced energy work and learned to produce sensations throughout much of my body through focused attention. I've explored yoga, qigong, Tibetan dream practices, occult traditions, lucid-dream techniques, and some of the ideas surrounding Robert Monroe's work.

But I have not traveled through other realms and returned knowing with certainty that my consciousness left my physical body.

That is part of why I want to do this.

If other people say they have learned to do something extraordinary through practice, what happens if I seriously try the practices myself?

Maybe astral projection is something distinct from dreaming.

Maybe it is an unusual form of lucid dreaming.

Maybe consciousness can do things we don't yet understand.

Maybe the explanation will eventually seem completely ordinary.

I don't know.

I'd rather investigate than decide in advance.

This guide is partly for anyone else who shares that curiosity, and partly for me. I want a daily practice again. I want to gather useful techniques from different traditions, understand what science can actually support, discard what doesn't help, and find out what happens when I consistently practice.

## Early relationship with dreams

Some of my childhood dreams are more vivid in my memory than actual events from childhood.

In grade school, I remember telling a classmate about a nightmare involving a bear. At some point during the dream, I realized something simple: I could just tell the bear to stop.

So I did.

And it stopped.

I did not have language for lucid dreaming then, but the possibility that a person could become aware inside a dream was already there.

I also did not grow up being forced into one religious framework. That gave me room to explore different religions, philosophies, art, and ideas without feeling that one of them had to contain the whole answer.

William Blake's poetry and art became part of that curiosity.

## Science, reality, and wonder

Fritjof Capra's *The Turning Point: Science, Society, and the Rising Culture* was an important influence.

Ideas from physics opened a crack in what had seemed like ordinary reality. Questions about matter, particles, light, measurement, observation, and the double-slit experiment made the world feel much less settled than everyday common sense suggests.

Those ideas do not prove anything about astral projection.

They made me comfortable admitting that reality may not behave exactly the way it appears to.

## Waking Life and lucid dreaming

In my twenties I bought *Waking Life* at a pawn shop.

The film made lucid dreaming feel like something I wanted to deliberately explore rather than merely something strange that occasionally happened.

I tried techniques from the movie, but the practices that finally led to consistent progress came later when I began working through Robert Bruce and Brian Mercer's *Mastering Astral Projection*.

I was making steady progress through the book until my child was born.

Then my priorities changed.

My child is grown now, and I want to return to that daily exploration.

## First lucid-dream experiences

One of the first things that worked was practicing the idea of falling backward while dreaming and attempting to remain conscious through the sensation instead of waking up.

Flying proved much less cooperative.

I've had several dreams in which I knew I was dreaming and tried to fly with mixed results.

Once I was trying to escape an alligator infestation. I ran, jumped, successfully took off—and promptly flew into a light pole and woke myself up.

In another dream I managed to levitate, but only about a foot above the ground.

Another time I floated but couldn't figure out how to actually go anywhere. I was just hanging there, trying to scoot myself through the air.

Eventually things improved.

One of my favorite lucid dreams involved riding Falkor from *The NeverEnding Story*.

Lucid dreaming can be profound.

It can also be ridiculous.

Both are worth remembering.

## Shared dreaming as an open question

I have never knowingly met another lucid dreamer inside a dream and later confirmed that we shared the same experience.

I would very much like to know whether that is possible.

That kind of verification interests me because it moves the question beyond “Did something extraordinary happen inside my experience?” toward “Did two independent people obtain information that suggests something occurred outside either person's private experience?”

I would need more than my own experience before making strong claims about astral travel.

But even without that confirmation, lucid dreaming remains fascinating and worthwhile.

## Conscious while the body slept

One of my strongest experiences happened during a two-day event where several people were sleeping in the same room.

I became aware of an incredibly annoying sound.

I remember lying there wondering who was making that awful noise.

I sat up and looked around.

Everyone else appeared to be asleep.

Then I realized why the noise sounded so loud.

It was me.

I was hearing my own snoring.

The moment I understood what was happening, the experience collapsed and I woke normally.

It was also slightly embarrassing.

But it became an important moment for me.

Whatever the correct neurological explanation, my subjective experience was unmistakable: my consciousness felt awake while my body appeared to be sleeping.

That made the sleep/wake boundary much more interesting.

## Sleep paralysis

I have not experienced classic sleep paralysis.

I have had nightmares, but I have not experienced the familiar report of feeling fully awake while being unable to move.

The guide should never assume every participant experiences sleep paralysis or needs to experience it.

## Energy work

I took a weekly qigong class.

One of the first exercises involved imagining or feeling a ball of energy between the hands and working with that sensation.

I enjoyed the Eight Brocades movements.

At first I mostly noticed energy-like sensations in my hands.

Later, while practicing techniques from *Mastering Astral Projection*, I realized that the sensation I had learned to notice in my hands could apparently be produced throughout much more of my body through focused attention.

My ordinary description of the sensation is:

**Soft vibrating electricity. Fuzzy, tingling, sometimes with tiny sparks.**

I do not need to know exactly what it is in order to investigate it.

## Moving attention through the body

With practice, I became able to deliberately produce or notice these sensations where I focused.

For example, I could put my attention inside my big toe and imagine moving something like an energy sponge back and forth through it.

Then the other toes.

Then the foot.

The sensation could feel like waves moving internally.

From there I could move attention into the calf, thigh, and other areas.

Eventually I could feel this through most of my body.

The spine remained difficult.

That is still an area I want to explore, particularly because spinal energy imagery appears in kundalini and other traditions.

Practices involving the pelvic floor, breath, spinal attention, and upward-moving sensation interest me, but the guide should research their history, physiological implications, and safety rather than simply repeating traditional instructions as fact.

## Working definition of energy

For this project, “energy” does not need to begin as a supernatural claim.

It can begin with experience.

Put attention somewhere.

Do something deliberately with that attention.

Notice whether sensation changes.

If someone feels tingling, warmth, pressure, vibration, movement, or something difficult to name, that experience becomes something we can investigate.

Different traditions may call similar experiences qi, chi, prana, subtle energy, vital energy, or other names.

We do not have to choose one explanation.

A useful provisional definition for the book is:

**Energy is the felt experience of directed consciousness in the body.**

That is a working model, not a final scientific claim.

## The energy body

Astral traditions often describe some form of nonphysical, subtle, or energy body.

The theory is that during astral projection, the physical body remains where it is while consciousness operates through another body or form.

I don't know whether that is literally true.

But body awareness and directed attention are useful practices even before we get anywhere near that question.

Learning to relax deeply, become aware of subtle sensations, move attention deliberately, and observe the changing boundary between waking and sleeping may be valuable regardless of what theory ultimately explains the experiences.

## Yoga and body awareness

Yoga is useful to me without requiring a supernatural explanation.

It helps with basic care of the body, flexibility, relaxation, and learning to remain calm while holding physically difficult positions.

That combination of exertion, attention, and relaxation connects naturally with the larger experiment.

## Tibetan dream practice

I have read *The Tibetan Book of Living and Dying* and *The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep* by Tenzin Wangyal Rinpoche, edited by Mark Dahlby.

*The Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep* made a strong impression because it suggested lucid-dream experiments I had never thought were possible: becoming multiple versions of yourself, becoming extremely large or small, becoming something else entirely, and deliberately testing the limits of identity and perception while lucid.

The book presents dream yoga and sleep yoga from Bön and Tibetan Buddhist traditions, using lucid dreaming not only for exploration but as a setting for deeper contemplative practice and inquiry into the nature of mind. Its spiritual framework should be represented accurately as the tradition's framework, not automatically adopted as scientific fact.

This is a priority source for later research and citation. Verify specific exercises and edition details against the book/publisher before quoting or attributing individual practices.

Some Tibetan practices also involve sleep position, intention, visualization, and ways of approaching dreams as part of consciousness training.

I do not remember one specific Tibetan technique producing a dramatic breakthrough for me.

That does not mean it failed.

Many of these practices depend on consistency, and consistency is one of my difficulties.

This project is partly an attempt to solve that.

## Monroe and altered-state methods

Robert Monroe's work interests me, particularly accounts of out-of-body states and shared or verifiable experiences.

I have read about the work but have not seriously completed Monroe-style programs, partly because much of the structured material costs money.

The book should distinguish between things I have personally practiced and things I have only read about.

I am also interested in binaural beats.

I enjoy them and would like to understand what research actually says about whether they meaningfully help relaxation, sleep-edge states, concentration, creativity, or other altered states.

If useful, I would eventually like to create our own.

## The sleep edge, creativity, and problem solving

The boundary between waking and sleep interests me for reasons beyond astral projection.

People have long reported creative insights around hypnagogia—the transitional period as waking thought begins turning into dreamlike imagery.

I am interested in stories of inventors, artists, scientists, and thinkers deliberately using that boundary to work on problems.

The guide should investigate:

- hypnagogia;
- dream-based problem solving;
- creativity during sleep transitions;
- memory and dreams;
- deliberate incubation of questions before sleep;
- historical techniques such as holding an object while drifting toward sleep so that dropping it triggers awakening.

These stories should be fact-checked rather than repeated from folklore.

## My position on astral projection

If someone tells me, “Astral projection is obviously nonsense,” my reaction is essentially:

Maybe.

But I haven't finished the experiment yet.

I am an agnostic in the broad Robert Anton Wilson sense. I am capable of considering a model as though it were true while also considering the possibility that it isn't.

I would rather practice seriously enough to experience what people are talking about before deciding that nothing is there.

And even then, my experience might not mean what I initially think it means.

## My position on certainty

If someone tells me astral projection is absolutely real and explains exactly how the universe works, I don't feel the need to argue with them either.

If their experience is real to them, I can respect that.

I want to know:

What happened?

What did you practice?

How did it feel?

Can it be repeated?

Can anything about it be independently verified?

I can be excited about someone's experience without adopting their explanation.

## Belief as an experiment

Western occult traditions also contain deliberately strange approaches to belief.

One idea associated with ceremonial occult practice is intentionally inhabiting a belief system deeply, experiencing what happens from inside it, and later stepping outside it again.

That interests me.

Belief itself may affect attention, expectation, perception, and behavior.

Rather than pretending we have none, *Psychical Excursion* can occasionally treat models as tools:

Try the model.

Do the practice.

Observe.

Then step back.

What happened?

## Why astral travel?

Curiosity.

Understanding consciousness.

Adventure.

And, very simply:

**If other people can do this, what am I missing?**

What is possible?

Is there more to existence than what we normally perceive?

The psychonaut impulse is important to the voice of this book.

Exploration is worthwhile partly because exploring is fun.

## What would convince me?

My own extraordinary experience would not be enough for complete certainty.

The most compelling evidence would involve independently verifiable information.

For example:

Two people believe they met during a dream or out-of-body experience.

They later compare what happened without coordinating beforehand.

Both remember details that are difficult to explain through chance or prior knowledge.

That would interest me enormously.

Until then, an experience can still be meaningful, strange, useful, funny, transformative, or beautiful without proving a metaphysical theory.

## Practice problem: consistency

My biggest obstacle is not necessarily technique.

It is consistency.

I am naturally scattered.

I jump between interests and projects.

Life interrupts routines.

That happened with my earlier astral-projection practice when my child was born, for a very good reason.

Now I want a structure that makes daily practice easier to return to.

The 60-day guide should therefore help someone continue without turning practice into another obligation they can fail.

No grades.

No streak shame.

No checkmarks required.

Miss a day?

Come back.

Can't remember a dream yet?

Keep reading.

Feel energy immediately?

Interesting.

Feel absolutely nothing?

Also interesting.

People move at different speeds.

The guide is a path, not a test.

## Things that make me stop reading

Avoid:

- excessive mysticism presented as established fact;
- excessive skepticism that dismisses experience before investigating it;
- preaching;
- “this is the only way” language;
- gurus speaking with absolute certainty;
- jargon for its own sake;
- rigid programs that turn curiosity into homework;
- repetitive commands;
- treating one person's experience as universal;
- pretending uncertainty is a weakness.

Prefer:

- curiosity;
- good stories;
- real research;
- historical context;
- experiments;
- questions;
- practical exercises;
- multiple possible explanations;
- gentle humor;
- permission to notice nothing;
- useful techniques even when the extraordinary claim remains unresolved.

## Humor

Humor should be mild and human.

*Psychical Excursion* itself sounds a little funny, and that is okay.

The name was originally chosen pragmatically while looking for astral-projection-adjacent language with an available domain, but it grew on me because it sounds historical, playful, and adventurous.

There is also a deliberately unserious kernel underneath it: I want psychic super powers. I want an adventure.

Examples of the appropriate register:

- If all you notice is that your arm is getting tired, congratulations: you noticed something.
- Apparently lucid-dream flight also has a learning curve. I once escaped an alligator infestation only to fly directly into a light pole.
- Becoming conscious while your body sleeps sounds mysterious until the great revelation is discovering that the horrible noise in the room is your own snoring.

Humor should release tension, not turn the subject into a joke.

## Two-minute explanation

If someone asked what this project is over coffee:

I think there is more to this world than meets the eye. I want to explore it. I want to learn more and actually try the methods that might get me there.

We spend so much of our lives sleeping. What if that time can be used for more than just being unconscious?

What if it's a little like *The Matrix* and I can learn kung fu in my sleep?

The point is not to promise that anything supernatural will happen. The point is to explore dreams, attention, consciousness, energy work, lucid dreaming, and out-of-body experience seriously enough to find out what is actually possible.

## Preferred framing: out-of-body experience

“Out-of-body experience” is the preferred term when discussing the larger phenomenon, while *astral projection* and *astral travel* remain useful historical and cultural terms.

OBE connects naturally to questions about lucid dreaming, near-death experiences, trauma, time perception, and the nature of consciousness.

A personal example: I survived a car wreck in which the vehicle flipped completely over. During the event, time seemed to slow dramatically.

What is that?

Is it an effect of stress chemistry and brain processing?

Is DMT relevant to some extreme states, or is that overstated?

Is there something more going on?

How does the brain construct our experience of time?

Is consciousness entirely produced by the body, or can it ever operate independently?

These are the kinds of questions that belong in *Psychical Excursion*.

Personal events should remain personal narrative; physiological and scientific explanations must be independently researched before publication.

## Author presence

The author's story should emerge lightly through the book rather than dominate the opening.

Use personal experiences when they illuminate the day's question, practice, or uncertainty.

The author is not the guru. He is another participant with some prior experience who is returning to the experiment.

## What I hope Day 60 gives the reader

I hope they discover more about themselves and find a new love of life and what is possible.

Success does not require proving astral projection.

A reader might finish with better dream recall, deeper relaxation, stronger attention, a lucid dream, a richer relationship with sleep, more body awareness, an OBE-like experience, or simply more curiosity.

The desired result is not certainty.

It is discovery.

## Narrative posture

The book should frequently move through this pattern:

**Question → story or claim → research → experiment → observation → another question.**

Not:

**Claim → command → command → command.**

Useful openings:

- Have you ever noticed…?
- What would happen if…?
- What exactly do people mean when they say…?
- Is it possible that…?
- Before deciding, can we test some part of it?
- Different traditions describe this differently.
- Science can tell us something here, although perhaps not everything.
- Here's the part we can actually try.
- What do you notice?

## The reader relationship

The author is not above the reader.

The author is participating too.

The implicit relationship is:

**I'm exploring this with you. I've experienced some strange things, but I don't have all the answers. Let's look at what others have discovered, separate claims from evidence where we can, and actually try the practices instead of arguing about them from a distance.**

## Central question

*Psychical Excursion* ultimately asks:

**What are our minds and bodies capable of when we deliberately train attention, dreams, relaxation, body awareness, intention, and consciousness—and how far can that exploration really go?**

Maybe lucid dreaming is the destination for some people.

Maybe deeper meditation is enough for others.

Maybe somebody experiences something they call astral projection or an out-of-body experience.

Maybe what we discover challenges the words we started with.

We won't know what happens to us unless we try.
